Introduction to Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is a powerful AI framework that enhances large language
models (LLMs) by connecting them to external knowledge bases. Unlike purely parametric models
that rely solely on information baked into their weights during training, RAG systems can
dynamically retrieve relevant information at inference time.

How RAG Works

The RAG pipeline consists of two main phases:

1. Indexing Phase
   During indexing, documents are loaded, split into chunks, embedded using a vector
   embedding model, and stored in a vector database (such as FAISS, Pinecone, or Chroma).
   The embeddings capture the semantic meaning of each chunk.

2. Retrieval and Generation Phase
   When a user submits a query, the system:
   a) Embeds the query using the same embedding model.
   b) Searches the vector store for the most similar document chunks.
   c) Augments the LLM prompt with the retrieved context.
   d) Generates a grounded, factual answer.

Key Benefits of RAG

- Reduced Hallucinations: By grounding responses in retrieved facts, RAG significantly
  reduces the tendency of LLMs to fabricate information.

- Up-to-Date Knowledge: The vector store can be updated without retraining the LLM,
  making RAG suitable for rapidly changing domains.

- Source Attribution: RAG systems can cite the documents used to generate an answer,
  improving transparency and trust.

- Cost Efficiency: Fine-tuning LLMs is expensive. RAG achieves domain adaptation
  through retrieval at a fraction of the cost.

Advanced RAG Techniques

Modern RAG systems go beyond naive retrieval:

- HyDE (Hypothetical Document Embeddings): Generate a hypothetical answer, embed it,
  and use that to retrieve actual documents.

- Multi-hop Retrieval: Chain multiple retrieval steps to answer complex questions.

- Re-ranking: Use a cross-encoder model to rerank initially retrieved documents.

- Self-RAG: The model decides when to retrieve and evaluates its own outputs.

- FLARE (Forward-Looking Active REtrieval): Retrieve proactively when the model is
  uncertain about the next tokens.

Chunking Strategies

The choice of chunking strategy significantly impacts retrieval quality:

Fixed-size chunking is simple and predictable but may split semantic units.
Recursive splitting respects paragraph and sentence boundaries.
Semantic chunking groups text by meaning, producing the most coherent chunks.
Sentence window chunking creates fine-grained chunks with surrounding context.

Vector Stores

FAISS (Facebook AI Similarity Search) is a highly efficient library for similarity search
and clustering of dense vectors. It supports exact and approximate nearest neighbor search
and scales to billions of vectors.

Other popular vector stores include Chroma, Pinecone, Weaviate, and Qdrant.

Embedding Models

Quality embeddings are critical to RAG performance. Google's text-embedding-004 model
produces 768-dimensional embeddings and achieves state-of-the-art performance on the
MTEB benchmark. OpenAI's text-embedding-3-large and Cohere's Embed v3 are strong alternatives.

Conclusion

RAG represents a pragmatic approach to knowledge-augmented AI that combines the reasoning
capabilities of large language models with the precision of information retrieval systems.
As the ecosystem matures, we are seeing increasingly sophisticated RAG architectures that
address limitations like context window constraints, retrieval quality, and generation faithfulness.
